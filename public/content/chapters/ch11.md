# Chapter 11: Introduction to Bug Bounty Hunting

> **Learning objectives.** Explain what bug bounty programs and VDPs are; distinguish public/private programs and disclosure models; describe economics, severity, CVSS, CWE, CVE, triage, duplicates, and reputation; and communicate professionally.

## 11.1 What Bug Bounty Programs Are

A **bug bounty program** is an arrangement where an organization invites external researchers to find and report vulnerabilities in defined assets, offering recognition and/or rewards for valid findings. A **Vulnerability Disclosure Program (VDP)** typically accepts reports without monetary rewards. Both formalize the previously ad-hoc practice of contacting a vendor about a security issue.

Programs define:

- **Scope:** which assets and vulnerability types are in/out.
- **Rules of engagement:** what testing is allowed.
- **Submission process:** where and how to report.
- **Triage and rewards:** how findings are evaluated and compensated.

## 11.2 Public vs. Private Programs

- **Public programs:** anyone can register and submit. Often have broad scopes and high competition; duplicate rate is high.
- **Private programs:** invitation-only, limited researcher pools; less noise, often higher value findings.
- **Time-boxed events**: temporary programs with specific goals.

**Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD)** is the structured process of reporting, validating, fixing, and disclosing. ISO/IEC 29147 (disclosure) and ISO/IEC 30111 (handling) are the international references. Responsible disclosure means giving the vendor a reasonable remediation window before public discussion; many programs specify a disclosure timeline and require confidentiality until fixed.

## 11.3 Economics and Rewards

Rewards vary enormously by program, severity, asset criticality, and the organization's budget. Never rely on remembered figures — they change. Consult each program's published policy on the platform. Factors influencing reward:

- Severity and exploitability.
- Sensitivity of affected data.
- Quality of the report and PoC.
- Novelty and research effort.
- Whether the issue was previously unknown.

**Important:** income from bug bounty is highly variable and uncertain. Most researchers earn nothing in early months; many never earn significant amounts. Treat it as a skill-building activity with *possible* upside, not a reliable income source. This book makes no promise of earnings, rewards, certifications, or employment.

## 11.4 Severity, CVSS, CWE, CVE

- **CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System):** an open standard (currently CVSS v4.0) producing a 0--10 score from base, temporal, and environmental metrics. Learn to compute base scores, but remember programs often use their own business-context severity.
- **CWE (Common Weakness Enumeration):** a taxonomy of software weakness types (e.g., CWE-79 XSS, CWE-89 SQLi, CWE-639 IDOR). MITRE maintains it.
- **CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures):** an identifier for a *specific* published vulnerability (e.g., CVE-2021-44228). Program findings in web apps usually do not receive CVEs.
- **Security advisory:** a vendor publication describing a vulnerability, affected versions, and fixes.

Severity classification typically considers impact (confidentiality/integrity/availability), attack vector, complexity, privileges required, user interaction, and scope. A self-XSS that affects only the attacker is usually Informative; stored XSS affecting all users can be High.

## 11.5 Triage, Duplicates, and Outcomes

Typical report states:

- **New / Triage:** awaiting initial review.
- **Valid / Accepted:** confirmed; bounty decision pending.
- **Duplicate:** the same issue was already reported; usually no reward.
- **Informative / Not applicable / Not a vulnerability:** no security impact or out of scope.
- **Out of scope:** asset or type not covered.
- **Resolved / Fixed:** remediation deployed; retest may be allowed.
- **Disclosed:** public after coordination.

**Common rejection reasons:** out of scope, duplicate, no security impact (e.g., version banner disclosure), self-only issues, requires unlikely preconditions, theoretical without PoC, caused by the researcher's own actions (e.g., self-DoS), or violates program rules.

Professional response to rejection: read the triage comment, ask a concise clarifying question if needed, and move on. Arguing without new evidence damages reputation.

## 11.6 Researcher Reputation

Platforms track signal: valid report rate, duplication rate, clarity, and responsiveness. A strong reputation leads to private invitations and faster triage. Build it by submitting only well-validated, in-scope, clearly written reports and by treating triage staff professionally.

## 11.7 Professional Communication

- Use clear, concise language; avoid exaggeration.
- State preconditions and limitations honestly.
- Provide reproducible steps and evidence.
- Respect disclosure timelines and confidentiality.
- Never threaten, demand, or publicize prematurely.

## 11.10 Mastery Deep Dive: The Economics of Bug Bounty

> **Authorization and accuracy note.** Every example and exercise in this deep dive assumes either an asset you own, a local lab, or an asset explicitly listed as in scope in a current, written program policy. Reward values are expressed as relative *reward units* rather than currencies because published amounts change; the program page is always authoritative.

### 11.10.1 The Principal-Agent Problem Behind Every Program

A bug bounty program is a market for *private security information*. The organization (the principal) cannot directly observe how much effort a researcher (the agent) invests, so it pays only for information that survives triage. This single fact explains most of the friction researchers experience:

| Stakeholder | What they want | What creates friction |
| — | — | — |
| Researcher | Recognition, reward, learning, reputation | Unpaid effort on duplicates and out-of-scope findings |
| Triage analyst | Clean, reproducible, correctly classified reports | Ambiguous impact, missing steps, "theoretical" issues |
| Program owner | Fewer real incidents, cost predictability | Noise, reputational risk, disclosure surprises |
| Platform | Healthy marketplace, fast resolution | Disputes, low-quality submission volume |

Because effort is unobservable, programs substitute *signals*: scope boundaries (to reduce off-target effort), safe harbor (to reduce the researcher's risk), triage SLAs (to make the market credible), and reputation systems (to reward researchers historically associated with clean, valid reports). An elite researcher internalizes this: your competitive advantage is not "trying harder," it is producing a higher *signal density* — valid, in-scope, well-evidenced findings per hour of effort.

**Common mistake.** Treating a program as a lottery and maximizing the number of submissions. Each weak report spends triage attention and your own reputation budget. In a signal-driven market, volume without validation is a negative-value activity.

### 11.10.2 A Program Maturity Model

Programs are not interchangeable. Diagnosing maturity lets you predict how your effort will be treated before you invest it. Use this five-level model as a working heuristic, not a formal standard:

| Level | Name | Scope & policy | Triage behavior | Researcher strategy |
| — | — | — | — | — |
| 1 | Ad-hoc | Vague scope, no safe harbor, no disclosure terms | Slow or silent; reports closed without explanation | Avoid until clarity is given in writing |
| 2 | Reactive | Published scope, basic exclusions, reward ranges | Inconsistent classification; frequent informative closures | Small time investment; test only unambiguous assets |
| 3 | Defined | Written rules, test accounts, explicit exclusions, disclosure timeline | Consistent taxonomy; stated response targets | Good default choice; standardize your workflow here |
| 4 | Managed | Scope map, rate limits, named contact, retest rights, public statistics | Fast, reasoned triage with dialogue | Invest deeply; build reputation and private invitations |
| 5 | Optimized | Staged scope releases, dedicated triage team, feedback loop, coordinated disclosure culture | Collaborative; clarifications before closure | Long-term partnership; highest-value, lowest-friction work |

**How to diagnose maturity without inside information.** Read the public disclosure archive if the program allows one. Look for (a) the median time from submission to first response, (b) whether triage comments explain *why* a report was closed, (c) whether repeat researchers appear to have their reports accepted, and (d) whether the policy names a contact path for scope questions. A program that answers questions publicly before testing begins is demonstrating Level 4 behavior. A program whose policy says "we reserve the right to change scope without notice" is signaling Level 1 risk.

**Annotated example.** Program A publishes `*.example-lab.test`, excludes third-party-hosted subdomains, provides two test accounts, and has a public disclosure log showing reasoned triage notes. Program B publishes "all our assets" with no exclusions and no safe harbor. Program A is the better investment even if Program B advertises a higher maximum reward, because B's ambiguity converts your effort into out-of-scope rejections.

### 11.10.3 Expected Value of a Testing Session

Treat each engagement as a portfolio of hypotheses rather than a single lottery ticket. A simplified model:

```text
EV(session) = E[ sum over hypotheses i of (P(valid_i) x reward_i) ]
             - (hours x your_hourly_value)
             - (tooling and lab cost)
             - (reputation cost of weak reports)
```

The terms that are under your control are `P(valid_i)` and the number of hours. `reward_i` is set by the program. This is why the highest-leverage skill is *hypothesis quality*: a small number of well-formed, impact-backed hypotheses beats a large number of speculative probes.

Worked example with symbolic values (no currency implied): you have 12 available hours and three candidate hypotheses on an authorized target. Hypothesis H1 (object-level authorization on a documented API endpoint) has high prior probability because the endpoint takes a client-supplied object ID; H2 (reflected client-side script sink) has medium prior because input is encoded in the UI; H3 (banner disclosure) has near-zero value because the program classifies it informative. Allocating 6 hours to H1, 4 to H2, and 0 to H3 yields a higher expected value than spreading 4 hours across all three, even before rewards are considered. **Write the prior probability down before you test**; updating it afterward is how you calibrate.

The full conversion funnel for a personal pipeline is:

```text
in-scope assets -> live hosts -> interesting endpoints -> testable hypotheses
   -> candidate anomalies -> validated findings -> accepted reports -> rewards
```

Instrument your own journal with a count at each stage. After ten engagements you will know your personal conversion ratios, and you can direct effort at the stage with the largest drop. If 80% of your candidate anomalies become duplicates, the problem is recon freshness or target saturation, not report writing. If half your accepted findings are downgraded at triage, the problem is impact articulation.

### 11.10.4 Decision Tree: Where to Invest Time

Use this as an explicit decision procedure at the start of every week. Every branch assumes you have written authorization for the candidate target.

```text
Is there a current, written policy with a safe harbor?
   no  -> do not test. Request clarification in writing first.
   yes -> Is the specific asset explicitly in scope (or unambiguously
          covered by a wildcard whose depth is defined)?
             no  -> ask the program; do not assume.
             yes -> Can you operate within the stated technique
                    restrictions (no DoS, no social engineering, data limits)?
                       no  -> skip this program for now.
                       yes -> Is the technology within your current skill set
                              OR is learning it an explicit goal?
                                 no  -> deprioritize unless it is a deliberate
                                        stretch goal with a time box.
                                 yes -> Is the program mature enough (11.10.2)
                                        to give feedback you can learn from?
                                           no  -> cap time at a small block.
                                           yes -> invest a deep work block.
```

Notice that the first three branches are *authorization* branches, not profitability branches. This ordering is the professional standard: legality and scope precede economics.

### 11.10.5 Transaction Costs and Opportunity Cost

Every engagement carries costs that do not appear on a reward table:

- **Context-switching cost.** Re-learning a new application's architecture costs more than an extra hour of testing the one you already understand. Batch related work.
- **Tooling maintenance cost.** A pipeline of ten half-configured tools produces worse results than three well-understood ones. Automate only what you can debug.
- **Rejection cost.** A poorly written report consumes triage trust. Track your valid-report rate as a first-class metric.
- **Ethical cost.** Pressuring a program for a reward, testing an ambiguous asset, or touching real user data damages the entire community's access. These are hard stops, not tradeoffs.

**What mastery looks like.** You can explain, for any hour you spend, which funnel stage it advanced and why. You choose programs using a repeatable maturity rubric. You can say "no" to a high advertised reward when the scope or safe harbor is unclear, and you can articulate that decision calmly.

## 11.11 Mastery Deep Dive: Disclosure Models and the Legal Framework

### 11.11.1 A Disclosure Model Comparison Table

"Disclosure model" describes *who coordinates the fix and when the public learns*. These models overlap; a private program can still follow coordinated disclosure. Confusing them causes avoidable policy violations.

| Model | Who may report | Coordination | Publication default | Key researcher obligation |
| — | — | — | — | — |
| Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) | Anyone following the policy | Vendor and reporter agree on remediation window | After fix or by agreed date | Confidentiality until agreed milestone |
| VDP (no bounty) | Often anyone; defined channel | Vendor-led intake and triage | Varies; often a public acknowledgement page | Follow the stated channel and timing |
| Public bounty program | Registered researchers, open directory | Platform plus program triage | Program-controlled; disclosures often opt-in after fix | Respect platform and program disclosure rules |
| Private / invite-only program | Invited researchers only | Same as public, with tighter NDA norms | Usually restricted or embargoed | Treat invitations and findings as confidential |
| Time-boxed event | Registered participants during the window | Event organizers | Usually after event close, per rules | Stop testing when the window closes |
| Embargo / coordinated release | Named researchers or coordinators | Fixed publication date agreed in advance | Simultaneous release | Do not publish early, including hints |

ISO/IEC 29147 addresses disclosure, and ISO/IEC 30111 addresses vendor handling of vulnerabilities. They are reference frameworks, not a substitute for the program's own terms. Where the two conflict with a written program policy, the program policy governs your participation (and local law governs everyone).

**Common mistake.** Assuming "public program" means "public disclosure allowed." Publication rights are a separate clause. Many public programs prohibit any public discussion until they grant permission.

### 11.11.2 Anatomy of a Safe Harbor Clause

A safe harbor is the program's promise not to pursue certain legal action against researchers who comply with the policy. Read it as a contract with conditions, not a shield.

```text
A robust safe harbor answers:
  1. WHO is protected?   (named researchers? anyone acting in good faith?)
  2. WHAT conduct?       (testing within scope, using stated techniques)
  3. WHICH assets?       (the defined scope, including wildcard depth)
  4. WHAT conditions?    (data handling, no DoS, timely stop on harm)
  5. WHAT is excluded?   (third-party systems, out-of-scope assets, crimes)
  6. WHO binds it?       (the asset owner, or only a parent/affiliate?)
  7. WHAT law remains?   (statutes are not waived by a private clause)
```

**Annotated clause (fictional teaching example):**

> "We will not initiate legal action against researchers who, in good faith and in compliance with this policy, discover and report vulnerabilities in the in-scope assets. This authorization does not extend to any third-party service, to any asset not listed in scope, or to conduct that accesses, modifies, or retains data belonging to other users. This statement does not constitute consent to activity outside the scope described above."

Interpretation: protection is conditional on good faith and compliance; third parties and other users' data are expressly excluded; and the program disclaims broader consent. Note that "we will not initiate" binds only the issuing entity — it cannot bind a different subsidiary or a cloud provider.

**What mastery looks like.** You can quote the exact sentence that authorizes each testing action, and you stop when an action falls outside that sentence. When the clause is silent on a technique, you ask before acting.

### 11.11.3 Legal Landscape You Must Be Aware Of

This is general education, not legal advice. The point is to recognize that *policy permissions and statutory law are different layers*.

| Jurisdiction / regime | Instrument | Relevance to a researcher |
| — | — | — |
| United States | Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) | Unauthorized access to protected systems can be civil and criminal; "authorized" is a legal question, not just a policy label |
| United Kingdom | Computer Misuse Act 1990 | Unauthorized access and modification offenses; intent matters |
| India | Information Technology Act 2000 and amendments | Unauthorized access, data protection, and intermediary obligations |
| European Union / UK | GDPR / UK GDPR | Personal data encountered during testing must not be collected, stored, or exfiltrated; minimize and stop |
| Sectoral rules | HIPAA, PCI DSS, financial regulations | Breach reporting and data-handling duties can be triggered by unsafe testing |

Three practical consequences follow:

1. **Stay inside written authorization.** A safe harbor reduces risk; it does not amend a statute.
2. **Minimize data exposure.** If a response returns real user data, stop, do not save it, and report the observation without further access. Redact anything incidental in your notes.
3. **Do not improvise across borders.** An asset in another jurisdiction may be subject to laws you did not anticipate. Keep testing within the explicit program authorization and ask about cross-border scope if it is unclear.

> **Hard rule.** Never access, modify, exfiltrate, or destroy real user data. Never test an asset that is not in scope. Never run a technique the policy prohibits. These rules are absolute; they are not balanced against reward size.

### 11.11.4 Ethics Beyond the Letter of the Policy

Compliance is the floor, not the ceiling. Elite researchers apply a stronger test:

- **The "would I explain this in the report?" test.** If you would omit a testing action from your methodology section, do not perform it.
- **The "least impact" test.** Choose the PoC that demonstrates the boundary violation with the smallest side effect: read one synthetic object, not a table; write a harmless marker, not a record.
- **The "other people are users too" test.** Triage staff, support teams, and other researchers bear costs from noisy tests and frivolous reports. Respect their time.
- **The "no leverage" test.** Never threaten negative publicity to obtain a reward or faster response. Disclosure coordination is a professional process, not negotiation leverage.

**Common mistake.** Treating a bug bounty as a way to "teach the company a lesson." The relationship is a coordinated security partnership. Adversarial posturing closes programs and harms the community's access to legitimate work.

## 11.12 Mastery Deep Dive: Building a Sustainable Research Practice

### 11.12.1 Deliberate Practice, Not Repetition

Repeating what you already know produces comfort, not skill. Deliberate practice has four properties: a specific target skill, work just beyond your current ability, immediate feedback, and reflection that produces a changed behavior. Apply it as follows:

| Skill | Deliberate practice drill | Feedback source |
| — | — | — |
| Scope reading | Rewrite a real policy as a one-page scope map, then have a peer find omissions | Peer review; program clarification response |
| Hypothesis formation | Write three testable hypotheses per endpoint before touching a request | Compare with what you actually tested |
| Impact articulation | Draft the impact paragraph before building the PoC | Triage outcome and any request-for-information notes |
| Tool fluency | Reproduce the same recon result with two different tools | Diff of outputs |
| Reporting | Rewrite an old report after reading its triage outcome | Accepted/duplicate/informative classification |

Each drill has a *deliverable*, not just an activity. "I browsed some reports" is not practice; "I rewrote two reports and logged three specific changes" is practice.

### 11.12.2 The Research Journal as an Instrument

Your journal is the single highest-leverage artifact in a sustainable practice. It should contain, per session:

```markdown
# Session Journal Template

- Date / time box:
- Program and policy version read (URL + date):
- Authorized assets touched (list exact hosts):
- Scope question asked / answer received:
- Hypotheses (H1...) with prior confidence:
- Tools and exact commands used (rate limits noted):
- Observations and raw evidence locations (local paths only):
- Candidate findings and their validation status:
- Data-safety decisions (accounts used, markers, any stop-on-real-data event):
- Outcome / triage notes:
- What I would do differently next session:
```

Two rules make the journal powerful. First, record **prior confidence before testing**; otherwise hindsight rewrites your intuition and you never calibrate. Second, store evidence locally with timestamps and never paste real user data into it. A journal that omits authorization and data-safety fields is an incomplete professional record.

### 11.12.3 Time Allocation and Focus

Elite researchers protect deep work and timebox exploration:

- **Fixed deep blocks.** Two to four uninterrupted hours on one hypothesis cluster beats scattered short sessions.
- **Exploration cap.** Give a new tool or novel target class a fixed budget (for example, one block). If it produces nothing, stop or re-plan rather than drifting.
- **Stop-loss on saturation.** If several hypotheses on an asset are all duplicates, the asset is likely heavily tested. Rotate to a fresher in-scope asset rather than grinding.
- **Maintenance window.** Reserve a small, separate block for tool updates, reading release notes, and journal review. Never do this during a deep block.

### 11.12.4 Rejection, Burnout, and the Psychological Game

Most reports are closed without a reward, and most sessions produce nothing. This is normal market behavior, not a verdict on your ability. Practical guards:

- **Separate identity from outcome.** A duplicate is information about timing, not about you.
- **Keep a learning ledger.** Record one transferable lesson per session, even from a zero-result day. After six months this ledger is a more honest measure of growth than a reward total.
- **Do not chase.** Re-reading a closed report repeatedly, arguing without new evidence, or testing the same asset harder to "prove" a point are all signs of a bad loop. Ask one concise clarifying question, then move on.
- **Know the exit conditions.** If a session makes you want to bend a scope rule, stop immediately. Stress is a leading indicator of ethical drift.

### 11.12.5 Common Career Traps

| Trap | Why it is seductive | Why it fails | Better alternative |
| — | — | — | — |
| Reward-chasing | Big advertised maximums are visible | High variance; ignores conversion and scope quality | Score programs with the maturity rubric |
| Tool collecting | New tools feel like progress | Unmaintained pipelines produce unreliable results | Master three tools deeply, add one at a time |
| Volume submission | Easy to count | Damages reputation; spends triage trust | Submit only fully validated, in-scope findings |
| Public-disclosure pressure | Feels like recognition | Violates embargo and destroys access | Request permission and wait for coordination |
| "One more request" escalation | Curiosity and sunk cost | Leads to scope violations and safety incidents | Timebox and stop at the planned boundary |
| Copying writeups without modeling | Fast to consume | Produces pattern-matching, not understanding | Reproduce the method in a lab, then explain it |
| Ignoring the basics | Advanced topics are exciting | Most real findings come from solid fundamentals | Keep a fundamentals drill in weekly rotation |

### 11.12.6 A 90-Day Development Plan

The following plan assumes a lab or owned assets for all practice; any live testing occurs only in explicitly in-scope programs.

```text
Weeks 1-2   Foundations and ethics
            - Read the authorized program policy workflow end to end.
            - Build a scope-map template and a journal template.
            - Drill: rewrite three fictional policies into scope maps.

Weeks 3-4   Recon discipline (owned assets / lab)
            - Build a passive-first asset inventory for one owned domain.
            - Drill: reproduce the same host list with two tools and diff.

Weeks 5-8   Manual testing loop (lab)
            - For one lab app, map all endpoints and write a hypothesis
              per endpoint before testing.
            - Drill: full authorization matrix, two synthetic accounts.

Weeks 9-10  Evidence and reporting
            - Write two triage-ready reports from lab findings.
            - Peer-review for reproducibility and impact clarity.

Weeks 11-12 Integration and measurement
            - One authorized program engagement with strict timebox.
            - Compute your funnel conversion numbers and adjust the plan.
```

Success criteria are behavioral: you can produce a scope map, a journal, an evidence bundle, and a report that a peer can reproduce without asking you a question.

## 11.13 Mastery Deep Dive: Measuring Real Skill

### 11.13.1 Signal Metrics vs. Vanity Metrics

| Metric | What it suggests | Limitation |
| — | — | — |
| Number of reports submitted | Activity | Strongly misleading; duplicates and informatives inflate it |
| Valid / accepted report rate | Hypothesis and validation quality | Small samples are noisy; track over time |
| Duplicate rate | Recon freshness and target saturation | A high rate may mean a popular target, not poor skill |
| Median time to first triage response | Program maturity | Not under researcher control |
| Reproducibility score (peer can replay report) | Evidence and writing quality | Requires peer review to measure |
| Number of distinct vulnerability classes found | Breadth of mental model | Should be balanced with depth |
| Number of lab reproductions of published techniques | Applied understanding | Only counts if you can explain why it works |
| Scope questions asked before testing | Ethical judgment | Should be low if policies are clear, high value when ambiguity exists |

The two most honest personal metrics are **valid-report rate over a rolling window** and **peer reproducibility of your reports**. Everything else is context.

### 11.13.2 A Competence Rubric Across the Pipeline

Use this rubric to locate your current level at each stage and choose the next drill.

| Stage | Novice | Competent | Advanced | Mastery |
| — | — | — | — | — |
| Program selection | Picks highest advertised reward | Reads scope and exclusions | Scores maturity and conversion | Declines ambiguous programs without hesitation |
| Scope analysis | Reads domain list only | Handles wildcards and obvious exclusions | Detects third-party hosting and acquisition gaps | Writes a scope map others rely on |
| Recon | Runs one subdomain tool | Builds passive-to-active inventory | Correlates CT, DNS, JS, and historical data | Maintains continuous monitoring with diffing |
| Hypothesis | Probes randomly | States a hypothesis per endpoint | Prioritizes by trust boundary and impact | Updates priors explicitly from results |
| Validation | Relies on tool output | Confirms with second request and manual replay | Demonstrates minimal-impact boundary violation | Anticipates triage objections before writing |
| Data safety | Unclear on test accounts | Uses own accounts and markers | Stops on real data; redacts notes | Designs PoCs that cannot touch real data |
| Reporting | Lists steps only | Adds impact, evidence, remediation | Tailors severity to business context | Reports are replayable without author present |

### 11.13.3 Calibration: Duplicates, Informatives, and Retests

Duplicates and informative closures are *calibration data*. For each one, answer three questions in your journal:

1. **What signal did I ignore?** (e.g., the endpoint was recently disclosed publicly.)
2. **What assumption was wrong?** (e.g., I assumed a wildcard covered a third-party host.)
3. **What will I change?** (e.g., check public disclosure dates during recon.)

A retest after remediation is especially valuable: it teaches you how fixes are implemented and which bypasses are real vs. theoretical. Only retest when the program allows it and within the same scope.

### 11.13.4 Knowledge Check

Answer each question before reading the answers.

1. A program advertises a very high maximum reward but its policy has no safe harbor and does not define wildcard depth. What is the professionally correct first action?
2. A public program's policy says "public disclosure is at the program's discretion." You found a valid issue. What may you publish?
3. Your PoC returns one row containing a real customer name. What do you do with that row?
4. You receive a duplicate on a finding you spent six hours on. Name two useful journal entries.
5. Which two metrics are the most honest measures of personal skill, and why are report counts weaker?
6. A safe harbor says the company "will not pursue legal action against good-faith researchers who follow this policy." Does this authorize testing a branded subdomain hosted by an unrelated vendor? Why or why not?

### 11.13.5 What Mastery Looks Like: A Final Checklist

```text
[ ] I can state, from memory, the difference between CVD, VDP, private,
    public, time-boxed, and embargoed models.
[ ] For any engagement, I can point to the exact policy sentence that
    authorizes each action I take.
[ ] I decline ambiguous scopes and unsafe techniques without negotiation.
[ ] I never touch real user data and I stop when I encounter it.
[ ] I keep a journal with priors, evidence locations, and a lesson per session.
[ ] I choose programs using an explicit maturity rubric, not reward headlines.
[ ] My reports are reproducible by a peer without my involvement.
[ ] I treat duplicates and informatives as calibration data.
[ ] I can explain the expected-value reasoning behind where I spend my hours.
[ ] I understand that safe harbor is conditional and does not replace law.
```

Mastery in bug bounty is not a reward total. It is a reliable, ethical, repeatable practice in which scope discipline, impact reasoning, and clear communication make each hour of effort more valuable than the last.

## 11.8 Key Takeaways

- Bug bounty is a formalized, scoped research relationship — not permission to test anything.
- CVSS, CWE, and CVE describe different things; use them precisely.
- Duplicates and informative reports are normal; reputation comes from quality.
- Income is uncertain; prioritize learning and professionalism.
