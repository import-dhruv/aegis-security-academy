# Chapter 12: Bug Bounty Platforms

> **Learning objectives.** Describe the workflows of major platforms and programs; explain account setup, onboarding, scope interpretation, submission, triage, and reputation; and always consult official documentation rather than remembered policies.

> **Accuracy note.** Platform policies, reward ranges, and program scopes change frequently. This chapter describes *workflows and where to find authoritative information*, not current reward amounts. Always read the program page and official docs on the platform itself. Links below are official documentation entry points; verify them as of your reading date.

## 12.1 Common Workflow Across Platforms

Despite branding differences, most platforms share a flow:

1. **Create account** and complete researcher profile/onboarding (often including identity verification and a code of conduct).
2. **Discover programs** via a directory; filter by public/private, scope, and program type.
3. **Read the program brief**: scope, exclusions, rules, safe harbor, disclosure terms.
4. **Acknowledge rules** and sometimes complete training (e.g., HackerOne's training modules).
5. **Test strictly within scope**; keep a research log.
6. **Submit a report** with title, asset, steps, PoC, impact, and severity suggestion.
7. **Triage** by the program or platform; request more info if asked.
8. **Resolution**: accepted, duplicate, informative, fixed; bounty if applicable.
9. **Disclosure**: coordinated publication if allowed.

## 12.2 HackerOne

- **Docs:** https://docs.hackerone.com/
- **Programs:** https://hackerone.com/programs (directory; many private/invite-only).
- **Workflow:** researchers sign up, may need to complete onboarding/training, browse public programs, and submit via the program's page. HackerOne provides a reporting form with fields for vulnerability details, impact, and attachments. Bounties are paid through the platform; reputation is reflected in a researcher profile and signal metrics.
- **Notes:** safe-harbor language and disclosure terms vary by program. Check each program's policy and the platform's disclosure guidelines.

## 12.3 Bugcrowd

- **Docs:** https://researcher.bugcrowd.com/
- **Programs:** https://bugcrowd.com/programs
- **Workflow:** register, complete profile, opt into public programs or receive private invitations. Submissions use a structured form; Bugcrowd performs triage for many programs and uses its own vulnerability rating taxonomy (VRT) alongside program-specific severity.
- **Notes:** Bugcrowd emphasizes the VRT for consistent prioritization; read the program's scope carefully.

## 12.4 Intigriti

- **Docs:** https://www.intigriti.com/researchers (official researcher resources)
- **Programs:** https://www.intigriti.com/bug-bounty-programs
- **Workflow:** European-focused platform with public and private programs; register via official site, read program policies (scope, exclusions, rewards), and submit through the platform. Intigriti publishes researcher guidelines and often runs training events.

## 12.5 YesWeHack

- **Docs:** https://www.yeswehack.com/ (researcher documentation and program directory)
- **Workflow:** register, complete profile, join public programs or receive private invitations; submit via the platform. YesWeHack offers training resources and runs public programs; read each program's terms.

## 12.6 Synack

- **Docs:** https://www.synack.com/ (official researcher information)
- **Workflow:** Synack uses a vetted, application-based researcher network ("Synack Red Team"). Researchers apply, undergo screening, and then receive access to private programs through the platform. Testing often uses a dedicated virtual environment/VM provided by Synack.

## 12.7 Open Bug Bounty

- **Site:** https://www.openbugbounty.org/
- **Workflow:** a non-commercial disclosure platform focused on notifying site owners of vulnerabilities (commonly XSS) and coordinating disclosure. Rewards are generally not offered; it emphasizes responsible notification and transparency. Read its current disclosure policy before participating.

## 12.8 Vendor VDPs and Bug Programs

Many organizations run their programs on their own infrastructure. Always use official pages:

- **Google VRP:** https://bughunters.google.com/
- **Microsoft MSRC:** https://msrc.microsoft.com/report/ (and https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/bounty )
- **Apple Security Bounty:** https://security.apple.com/bounty/
- **Meta Bug Bounty:** https://www.facebook.com/whitehat/ and https://bounty.facebook.com/ (check current official page)
- **GitHub Security Bug Bounty:** https://bounty.github.com/
- **Cloudflare VDP:** https://www.cloudflare.com/disclosure/

For each: create the required account, read the current policy and scope (which often differs significantly from platform programs), follow the prescribed report format, and respect disclosure rules. Some programs require using specific test accounts or environments and prohibit certain test types (e.g., DoS, social engineering).

## 12.9 Choosing a Program

Criteria for beginners:

- Clear, broad scope with a safe harbor statement.
- Public program (no invitation needed).
- Well-written policy with explicit exclusions and rules.
- Responsive triage (look at public disclosures where available).
- Assets whose technology you understand.

Avoid programs with vague scope, no safe harbor, or rules that seem to require risky behavior. If in doubt, ask the program before testing.

## 12.10 Common Rejection Reasons (Recap)

Out of scope; duplicate; informative/no impact; self-only; theoretical; requires unauthorized data access; violates rules (e.g., scanning restrictions, DoS). Each platform's taxonomy differs slightly — read the triage documentation.

## 12.13 Mastery Deep Dive: Platform Mechanics and the Triage Pipeline

> **Accuracy and authorization note.** Platform features, policy templates, and reward ranges change. This deep dive describes durable mechanics and decision procedures; verify every specific against the platform's current official documentation. All testing described assumes an owned asset, a lab, or an explicitly in-scope program asset.

### 12.13.1 What a Platform Actually Does

A bug bounty platform is not merely a web form. It provides four services that shape researcher outcomes:

| Service | What it provides | Why it matters to you |
| — | — | — |
| Authorization record | Published policy, acknowledgement of rules, sometimes training completion | Establishes the written boundary of permission |
| Intake normalization | Structured fields for asset, vulnerability type, steps, impact, PoC | Makes your report classifiable; missing fields cause delays |
| Triage and escrow | Intermediary review, severity decisions, reward payment mechanics | Separates "found something" from "accepted finding" |
| Reputation memory | Per-researcher signal, valid-report history, private-invitation eligibility | Turns past quality into future access |

The practical implication: a platform's workflow is designed to convert your narrative into a *classification decision*. A report that maps cleanly onto the platform's fields (asset, type, impact, reproduction) is cheaper to triage and therefore more likely to be processed favorably. Fighting the form — for example, putting impact only in an attachment — adds friction at exactly the wrong moment.

### 12.13.2 Onboarding as a Professional Step

Onboarding is often treated as bureaucratic overhead. Treat it as the first control point of your engagement:

```text
[ ] Account created with a stable, professional identity.
[ ] Identity verification completed if required (do not impersonate others).
[ ] Code of conduct read and accepted.
[ ] Required training modules completed and understood, not just clicked.
[ ] Notification preferences set so you see requests for information.
[ ] Payout/profile details verified where applicable (no current figures assumed).
[ ] Program-specific acknowledgements recorded in your journal with dates.
[ ] Test-account instructions located and followed exactly.
```

**Common mistake.** Joining a program and beginning testing before saving a copy of the policy version you read. Policies change. Your journal should record the program URL, the date, and any explicit version or change note so that a later scope revision cannot retroactively make your work look unauthorized.

### 12.13.3 The Triage Pipeline Stage by Stage

Triage is a human process with incentives of its own. Understanding it lets you write reports that move through it instead of stalling.

| Stage | Who acts | What they decide | Their incentive | Your best action |
| — | — | — | — | — |
| 1. Intake check | Platform or automated filter | Completeness, obvious out-of-scope | Reduce queue noise | Fill every field; name the exact in-scope asset |
| 2. Preliminary review | Triage analyst | Is it a plausible security issue? | Fast, defensible classification | State impact in the first paragraph |
| 3. Reproduction | Triage analyst or program engineer | Does it reproduce as described? | Minimize back-and-forth | Provide exact requests, accounts, and markers |
| 4. Classification | Triage plus program policy | Valid / duplicate / informative / out of scope | Consistency with taxonomy | Pre-map your finding to the program's taxonomy |
| 5. Severity and reward | Program owner (sometimes platform-assisted) | Severity, bounty, disclosure handling | Budget predictability and business risk | Explain business impact, not just technical effect |
| 6. Remediation tracking | Program engineering | Fix and retest | Close findings safely | Offer a scoped retest if invited |
| 7. Disclosure | Program and researcher | Timing and publication | Controlled narrative | Respect embargo; ask, do not announce |

**Annotated example.** A report titled "XSS in search" with a screenshot and no request details reaches stage 2 but stalls at stage 3, because the analyst cannot replay it. A report titled "Stored script execution in search results, affects all authenticated users on in-scope host `app.example-lab.test`" with the exact POST body, the marker string used, and a statement that only synthetic test accounts were involved moves to stage 4 in one pass. The technical finding is identical; the *classification cost* is not.

### 12.13.4 Aligning With Triage Incentives

Triage analysts are rewarded for correct, consistent, low-ambiguity decisions. Align with that:

- **Lead with impact, then evidence.** The first two sentences should answer "what boundary is broken and who is affected."
- **Use the program's vocabulary.** If the policy uses its own severity matrix or references a taxonomy such as the Bugcrowd VRT, map your finding to it explicitly.
- **Make reproduction boring.** A triager should be able to follow numbered steps without creativity. Include exact URLs, parameters, account roles, and marker values.
- **Pre-empt the three common objections:** out of scope, already known, no real impact. Address each in one line where relevant ("Asset is listed under wildcard `*.example-lab.test`; no third-party hosting observed").
- **Respond once, precisely, to requests for information.** A single complete answer preserves momentum; fragmented replies restart the queue.

### 12.13.5 State Decision Tree for Researchers

```text
Report closed as ...
  DUPLICATE -> was the asset/issue truly the same?
                 yes -> log the signal (public disclosure date, saturation).
                 no  -> send ONE concise message with the distinguishing
                        evidence, then accept the program's decision.
  INFORMATIVE -> does the policy list this class as informative?
                   yes -> accept and adjust target selection.
                   no  -> ask one clarifying question with the specific
                          policy clause and your impact reasoning.
  OUT OF SCOPE -> re-check ownership and wildcard depth in the written
                  policy. Do not re-test the asset. Ask before future testing.
  NEEDS INFO  -> answer fully in one response with exact artifacts.
                  If you cannot provide it safely, say so and explain why.
  ACCEPTED    -> respect confidentiality; wait for coordination on disclosure.
```

> **Professional norm.** One clarifying question, then acceptance. Repeated argument without new evidence spends reputation and is the fastest way to lose future private invitations.

## 12.14 Mastery Deep Dive: Duplicates, Rewards, and Reputation

### 12.14.1 Duplicate Rules in Depth

A duplicate means the program already knew about the issue. Platforms differ in the details, so read the program's duplicate clause rather than assuming a universal rule.

| Duplicate policy variant | Meaning | Researcher implication |
| — | — | — |
| First valid report wins | Earliest accepted report receives the reward | Speed of validation matters, but only after safety and scoping |
| Disclosure-date cutoff | Issues already publicly disclosed are not eligible | Check public disclosure dates during recon |
| Known-issue exclusion | Program may reject issues in its existing backlog | Maintain a list of recently disclosed public issues per target |
| Platform-wide duplicate | Same issue across programs on the same platform | Note prior reports when testing related assets |
| Privileged-information clause | Insider or employee knowledge disqualifies a report | Do not use leaked or non-public internal information |

**A disciplined pre-submission duplicate check (on authorized targets only):**

```text
1. Search the platform's public disclosures for the asset and issue class.
2. Search public writeups and advisories for the same endpoint or parameter.
3. Read the program's "known issues" or "recently fixed" notes if provided.
4. Check whether the behavior depends on a configuration that was recently changed.
5. Record what you checked in the journal before submitting.
```

This check does not guarantee a unique finding, but it converts duplicates from baffling events into measurable market signals. A high duplicate rate usually means either a saturated asset or an incomplete recon source set.

### 12.14.2 Reward Calculation and Factors

Never quote current reward figures from memory. What you *can* master is the factor structure programs use:

| Factor | Typical effect on severity/reward | How to evidence it |
| — | — | — |
| Impact type | Confidentiality, integrity, availability; account takeover and data exposure usually rank highest | Demonstrate the boundary crossed with a minimal PoC |
| Scope of affected users | Self-only effects are typically informative; all-user effects are material | State which accounts/roles are affected using your own test accounts |
| Exploitability and preconditions | Fewer preconditions and lower complexity increase severity | List prerequisites honestly, including authentication level |
| Asset criticality | Production auth and payment paths outrank marketing pages | Reference the program's own asset tiering if published |
| Data sensitivity | Exposure of sensitive data raises impact | Describe the field type, never the real value |
| Novelty and research depth | Novel bug classes or chained issues may earn bonuses | Explain the reasoning chain in the report |
| Report quality | Clear reports reduce cost and can affect awards | Include reproduction steps, evidence, remediation advice |
| Program policy overrides | Some classes are excluded or capped | Quote the applicable clause |

Worked example in reward units: suppose a program's published matrix assigns a base value `B` for a given severity on a given asset tier, and applies a multiplier `m` for report quality. Your expected value for a candidate finding is `B x m`, but your *expected value before validation* is `P(valid) x B x m`. This is why a 90%-confidence medium issue can be a better use of an hour than a 10%-chance critical issue: the probability term dominates when it is small. Prior calibration (Chapter 11) therefore directly affects allocation.

### 12.14.3 Reputation and Private Invitations

Reputation is a memory of signal quality. It typically reflects valid-report rate, duplication rate, clarity, responsiveness, and conduct. It matters because it gates access:

```text
quality reports -> higher valid rate -> platform trust -> private invitations
     -> smaller researcher pools -> fresher attack surface -> better findings
     -> stronger reputation (positive loop)

rushed reports -> duplicates/informatives -> lower trust -> fewer invitations
     -> more crowded public targets -> more duplicates (negative loop)
```

**Behaviors that build reputation:** submitting only after local validation; stating preconditions honestly; answering a single clarification thoroughly; respecting embargo; acknowledging when you are wrong.

**Behaviors that destroy it:** arguing closures without evidence; publishing before coordination; mass-submitting low-effort findings; using unauthorized data or tools; asking for rewards outside the platform's process.

### 12.14.4 VDPs vs. Bounty Programs

| Dimension | VDP | Bounty program |
| — | — | — |
| Primary goal | Establish a reporting channel and trust | Purchase validated security research |
| Reward | Usually recognition only | Monetary or other compensation per policy |
| Scope | Often narrower or product-specific | Often detailed with asset tiers and exclusions |
| Triage expectations | May be slower; fewer dedicated staff | Usually structured; may include SLAs |
| Disclosure | Often encourages acknowledgement after fix | Usually program-controlled |
| Best use in your development | Practice communication, build relationships, learn a product | Validate findings and develop depth where rewards exist |

A VDP is not "lesser." For a learner it is a low-pressure environment to practice the *reporting* half of the craft, provided the channel is official and the scope is read. Never assume a VDP implies permission beyond its stated scope.

### 12.14.5 Writing Program Notes

Program notes are your private, structured memory of a program's rules and behavior. Keep them local, factual, and current.

```markdown
# Program Notes Template

Program / platform:
Policy URL (and date read):
Program type: public / private / VDP / time-boxed / Web3
Maturity estimate (Chapter 11 rubric):
In-scope assets (exact strings):
Wildcard depth and known exclusions:
Third-party hosts identified and their status:
Required test accounts and how obtained:
Technique restrictions (verbatim key sentences):
Rate limits / scanner approval requirements:
Data-handling rules:
Duplicate rule and known-issue clause:
Disclosure / embargo terms:
Severity taxonomy referenced:
Triage contact and clarification path:
Public disclosure archive checked (date):
Saturation assessment (recent reports/writeups observed):
Personal focus areas for this program:
Open scope questions and their answers (with dates):
```

**Worked example (fictional teaching data).** For `*.example-lab.test`, your note records that `support.example-lab.test` resolves to a third-party helpdesk and is excluded; that scanners require prior notification and a rate limit; that two labeled synthetic accounts are provided; that the policy defines wildcard depth as one label only; and that you asked whether `api.beta.example-lab.test` is covered and received written confirmation that it is. Six weeks later, when you return, this note prevents you from re-asking the question or, worse, assuming an answer was never given.

## 12.15 Mastery Deep Dive: Platform-Specific Ecologies

> Each subsection below describes durable workflow characteristics. Verify current features, program types, and documentation in the platform's official docs before acting.

### 12.15.1 HackerOne Mechanics

HackerOne hosts a large directory of public and private programs plus many VDPs. Key mechanics to master:

- **Program pages carry the authority.** Scope, exclusions, safe harbor, disclosure terms, and test-account instructions are per program, not global. Two programs on the same platform can have opposite rules on the same technique.
- **Training and onboarding gates.** Some programs require completing training before submission; completion is recorded. Do not treat this as a formality — it is part of the authorization record.
- **Reporting form fields.** Vulnerability details, impact, and attachments map directly to triage stages. Use the structure from 12.13.3.
- **Public disclosures.** Where a program permits them, disclosed reports are a free source of saturation data and writing models. Read the triage conversation, not just the summary.
- **Signal and reputation.** Researcher profile signals reflect historical report quality; these influence invitations and sometimes triage priority.

**Common mistake.** Assuming a "VDP" badge and a "bounty" badge on the same platform behave identically. They differ in reward expectations and sometimes in triage rigor; read the policy, not the badge.

### 12.15.2 Bugcrowd Mechanics

Bugcrowd is notable for the **Vulnerability Rating Taxonomy (VRT)**, a shared framework used to classify findings consistently across programs.

- **Taxonomy mapping is a skill.** Before submitting, identify the VRT category that best matches your finding and note it in the report. This reduces classification friction.
- **Program-specific severity still governs.** The VRT informs prioritization; the program's policy determines rewards and exclusions. Where they differ, the policy wins.
- **Triage models vary.** Some programs are triaged by Bugcrowd, others by the customer. Public disclosures and program notes reveal which model you are dealing with.
- **Prioritized / private programs.** Invitation-based access reduces noise. Reputation and clean report history are the route in.

### 12.15.3 Intigriti and YesWeHack Mechanics

These European platforms share characteristics worth noting without over-generalizing:

- **Regulatory context.** GDPR and local data-protection norms make data minimization especially salient. Programs commonly emphasize that real user data must not be accessed or retained.
- **Researcher guidelines and events.** Both platforms publish researcher resources and run training or time-boxed events. These are useful for deliberate practice (Chapter 11) when tested in labs.
- **Program terms vary by customer.** As elsewhere, the per-program policy governs scope, reward, and disclosure. Do not infer rules from the platform's general branding.
- **Language and jurisdiction.** Cross-border scope questions may arise; ask the program rather than assuming.

### 12.15.4 Immunefi and Web3 Programs

Web3 programs differ enough to warrant their own mental model. All guidance here assumes authorized programs, testnets, local forks, or explicitly in-scope test deployments — never live user funds or third-party protocols.

| Dimension | Traditional web program | Web3 / smart-contract program |
| — | — | — |
| Primary asset | Web app, API, infrastructure | On-chain contracts, protocol logic, sometimes front end and infra |
| Impact measure | Data and access boundary violations | Funds at risk, invariant violations, privilege escalation |
| Test environment | Staging or production test accounts | Local fork, testnet, or program-provided environment |
| PoC style | HTTP requests and screenshots | Transaction sequences, call traces, invariant-break demonstration |
| Severity framework | Program or platform matrix | Often a chain-specific severity classification (verify current docs) |
| Safety constraint | No real user data, no DoS | No real fund loss, no market manipulation, no unauthorized chain access |

**Safe Web3 workflow (authorized environments only):**

```text
1. Read the program's asset list: contract addresses, chains, and versions.
   Verify each address against an official source (docs, verified explorer).
2. Confirm which chains and deployments are in scope (mainnet fork vs testnet).
3. Clone the public repository and run the documented local test suite.
4. Build a local fork or use the provided testnet; never transact against
   real user funds or unrelated contracts.
5. Model invariants: what must always be true (balance conservation,
   access control, accounting consistency)?
6. Write a failing test that demonstrates an invariant break.
7. Report with the failing test, call trace, and a precise impact statement.
8. Do not liquidate, drain, front-run, or interact with real user positions.
```

**Common mistakes in Web3 programs:** testing a contract address that is not on the published asset list; using a mainnet transaction against live funds; treating a theoretical reentrancy without a reachable path as valid; ignoring the program's restriction on economic attacks such as oracle manipulation; and publishing a PoC before remediation.

### 12.15.5 Vetted Networks and Application-Based Programs

Some platforms use an application and screening process, then provide a dedicated environment or VM and private programs. Key implications:

- **Access is a privilege with conditions.** The screening process, NDA, and environment rules are part of authorization; violating them can end access permanently.
- **Dedicated environments change the rules.** Do not assume techniques allowed in the provided environment are allowed on a public asset. Scope is per engagement.
- **Tool discipline matters.** Using the prescribed environment protects both you and the program; improvising outside it is a boundary violation.

### 12.15.6 Platform Comparison at a Glance

| Platform family | Distinct mechanic | What to master first |
| — | — | — |
| HackerOne | Large public/private directory; per-program policies; public disclosures where allowed | Reading program pages and disclosure archives |
| Bugcrowd | VRT taxonomy and varied triage models | Mapping findings to VRT and program policy |
| Intigriti | European context, research resources, events | Data-handling norms and program-specific terms |
| YesWeHack | European context, training resources, public/private programs | Official researcher guidelines and per-program rules |
| Immunefi | On-chain asset lists and invariant-based severity | Local forks, invariant modeling, safe PoCs |
| Vetted networks | Application-based access and dedicated environments | Environment rules and engagement scope |
| Vendor VDPs | Company-run channels with bespoke formats | The vendor's exact policy and reporting format |

## 12.16 Mastery Deep Dive: Choosing Where to Invest

### 12.16.1 A Weighted Program Scoring Worksheet

Use a repeatable score instead of intuition. Assign each factor 0--5 and weight by your goals.

| Factor | Weight | Score (0-5) | Evidence used |
| — | — | — | — |
| Scope clarity and wildcard depth | 0.20 |  | Written policy review |
| Safe harbor and legal clarity | 0.15 |  | Exact clause review |
| Technique allowedness for your methods | 0.15 |  | Restrictions list |
| Triage responsiveness and reasoning | 0.15 |  | Public disclosure archive |
| Technology fit with your skills | 0.10 |  | Asset fingerprinting (authorized/passive) |
| Freshness / saturation | 0.10 |  | Recent public reports and writeups |
| Reward structure (relative, not current figures) | 0.10 |  | Published policy matrix |
| Data-handling feasibility (test accounts available) | 0.05 |  | Program instructions |
| **Total** | **1.00** |  |  |

Worked example: a program with perfect scope clarity (5), strong safe harbor (5), but zero compatibility with your required method (0) scores `5x0.20 + 5x0.15 + 0x0.15 + ...`. The zero on a heavily weighted constraint should force a hard stop regardless of the total — treat authorization and technique-fit as **gating criteria**, not tradeable points.

### 12.16.2 Saturation and Timing

Attack surfaces have a half-life. Findings cluster after scope expansions, new feature releases, and public disclosures. Practical tactics:

- **Watch for scope expansion.** A program that adds a new subdomain or API creates a fresh window. Confirm the change in the written policy before testing.
- **Read disclosure dates.** If a host was disclosed three weeks ago, expect duplicates; if it was added yesterday, expect novelty.
- **Track releases.** Public changelogs and documentation updates point to new code, which is where new bugs live.
- **Rotate assets.** When duplicate rate rises, move to a different in-scope asset rather than increasing volume on the saturated one.

### 12.16.3 Maintaining a Program Database

Your program notes (12.14.5) become more valuable as a searchable database:

```text
programs.csv columns:
name, platform, type, policy_url, date_read, scope_string,
wildcard_depth, exclusions, technique_limits, test_accounts,
duplicate_rule, disclosure_terms, saturation_notes, open_questions,
last_tested, next_review_date
```

Review quarterly. Delete notes that are no longer accurate; an outdated note is more dangerous than no note because it feels authoritative.

### 12.16.4 Common Platform Mistakes

- Treating platform-wide rules as replacing program-specific rules.
- Submitting before saving the policy version and test-account instructions.
- Assuming safe harbor language is identical across programs.
- Using a reward headline as the primary selection criterion.
- Ignoring the duplicate clause and public disclosure archive.
- Publishing a writeup before the program's disclosure window.
- Confusing VRT or severity-framework labels with program policy decisions.
- For Web3: testing addresses or chains not on the published asset list.

### 12.16.6 Knowledge Check

1. A program is on a platform with a well-known severity taxonomy, but its policy excludes a finding class that the taxonomy rates as high severity. Which governs, and why?
2. Your report is closed as a duplicate. Name three journal entries that make this useful.
3. What is the difference between a VDP and a bounty program in terms of goal and researcher strategy?
4. On a Web3 program, why is a local fork preferred over interacting directly with a deployed protocol?
5. A platform's global code of conduct and a program's policy appear to conflict on scanner use. What do you do?
6. Why is "first valid report wins" an incomplete description of duplicate handling?

<details>
<summary><strong>Answers</strong></summary>

1. The program's own policy governs. Taxonomies provide consistent classification language; the program policy defines scope, exclusions, and rewards for that specific program.
2. (a) Which public disclosure or prior report you missed; (b) the signal you ignored during recon; (c) one concrete process change, such as checking disclosure dates before testing.
3. A VDP's goal is a trusted reporting channel and relationship, often without rewards; it is useful for practicing communication and learning a product. A bounty program's goal is to purchase validated findings; it rewards depth and precision. Both require strict scope compliance.
4. A local fork lets you reproduce state-dependent behavior and demonstrate an invariant break without risking real funds, real users, or unrelated contracts. It also makes the PoC reproducible for triage.
5. Do not test until you have written clarification. Authorization must be unambiguous; conflicting rules mean you cannot rely on either as permission. Ask the program and record the answer with a date.
6. Because policies add variants: disclosure-date cutoffs, known-issue exclusions, platform-wide duplicates, and privileged-information clauses. Always read the specific duplicate clause.

## 12.11 Key Takeaways

- Workflows are similar: onboard → read scope → test within bounds → submit → triage → disclose.
- **Never** state current reward figures from memory; consult official program pages.
- Official documentation links are the authoritative source for policies and procedures.
- Reputation and quality matter more than volume of reports.
