# Chapter 13: Scope and Rules of Engagement

> **This is the most operationally important chapter in the book.** Read it before every testing session.

> **Learning objectives.** Parse program policies; identify in/out-of-scope assets, wildcard domains, third-party infrastructure, restrictions, and privacy limits; create test accounts safely; and internalize the authorization rule.

## 13.1 The Authorization Rule

> **Non-negotiable: never test systems without explicit authorization. Never access, modify, exfiltrate, or destroy other people's data.**

Authorization is:

- **Explicit** — granted by the owner or their delegated program.
- **In scope** — the asset is listed as in-scope in the current program policy.
- **Written** — a published program policy or written permission.
- **Bounded** — subject to the program's restrictions and time windows.

A public URL is not permission. A subdomain that "looks like it belongs" may be third-party hosted and out of scope. If you are unsure, **ask the program before testing**.

## 13.2 Anatomy of a Program Policy

A typical policy contains:

1. **Summary** — program goals and safe harbor.
2. **Scope** — in-scope domains/IPs/applications and wildcard rules.
3. **Out of scope** — exclusions (often third-party services, subdomains, APIs).
4. **Rules** — allowed/disallowed testing techniques.
5. **Rewards** — severity-based ranges (verify current figures on the program page).
6. **Disclosure** — confidentiality and publication terms.
7. **Test accounts** — how to obtain and use them.
8. **Contact** — who to ask for clarification.

## 13.3 Scope Interpretation

### In-Scope Assets

- Explicitly listed domains and applications.
- Wildcards: `*.example.com` usually means all subdomains at that level, but check whether it includes deeper levels (`a.b.example.com`) and whether certain subdomains are excluded.
- Mobile apps, APIs, and source code if listed.
- Specific IP ranges if listed.

### Out-of-Scope Assets

- Third-party infrastructure (e.g., a support desk hosted elsewhere), even if branded.
- Excluded subdomains (often `dev`, `staging`, or vendor-hosted).
- Assets of acquisitions or subsidiaries not listed.
- Products or vulnerability types explicitly excluded.

### Wildcards and Ambiguity

A wildcard can be deceptively broad. Example policy: "In scope: `*.labcorp.example`" with exclusions for "any asset not owned by Example Corp." A CNAME pointing to a SaaS provider is owned by the provider; if the policy excludes third-party services, do not test it. **When a domain resolves to infrastructure you cannot verify is owned by the program, ask.**

## 13.4 Testing Restrictions (Typical)

Programs commonly restrict or prohibit:

- **Automated scanning** that is high-volume or not rate-limited; some require prior approval for scanners.
- **Denial of service** — DoS/DDoS, resource exhaustion, spam, mass account creation.
- **Social engineering** — phishing employees, phone calls, physical intrusion.
- **Data access** — accessing, modifying, or exfiltrating real user data; use test accounts.
- **Production safety** — no destructive payloads, no persistence, no ransomware, no lateral movement.
- **Account limits** — create only the number of accounts needed; use provided test accounts.
- **Proof-of-concept limits** — no real exploit against other users; no XSS that steals real sessions; no mass data dumps.
- **Disclosure** — no public disclosure before coordination; no blogs/tweets with details until allowed.

## 13.5 Production Environment Safety

When testing production (only if in scope and allowed):

- Use your own test accounts only.
- Use harmless markers (e.g., "TEST-123") rather than real data.
- Avoid actions that affect other users or business operations.
- Keep request volumes low; respect rate limits.
- Stop immediately if you encounter real user data; note the observation and report without accessing it further.

## 13.6 Account Creation and Test Accounts

- Prefer official test accounts provided by the program.
- If self-registration is allowed, create the minimum needed and label them.
- Never use real identities or credentials of others.
- Do not use credential-stuffed or purchased accounts.
- Keep test accounts organized (the journal template in the handbook helps).

## 13.7 Proof-of-Concept Limitations

A good PoC:

- Uses your own accounts and synthetic data.
- Demonstrates the security boundary violation with minimal side effects.
- Shows impact (e.g., reads your second account's object) rather than dumping data.
- Is reproducible from the report.
- Does not leave persistent artifacts (files, accounts, backdoors) on production.

## 13.8 Responsible Disclosure Timelines

If no program policy exists, general responsible-disclosure practice:

1. Report privately to the vendor with clear details.
2. Provide reasonable time to fix (commonly 30--90 days, depending on complexity).
3. Keep communication professional; offer to retest.
4. Coordinate any public disclosure; avoid releasing exploit code before a fix is widely deployed.
5. If the vendor is unresponsive, consider a coordinator (e.g., CERT) and follow applicable law.

Programs replace this with their own explicit terms — follow those instead.

## 13.9 Sample Program Policy (Fictional Teaching Example)

> **Example Corp Bug Bounty Policy (fictional, for teaching)**
>
> **Scope:** `*.example-lab.test` and `api.example-lab.test`, including the public web application and its REST API. Mobile app (Android) version 2.x if distributed through official channels.
>
> **Out of scope:** `support.example-lab.test` (third-party helpdesk), `cdn.example-lab.test`, any asset not owned by Example Corp, physical premises, social engineering, and denial-of-service testing.
>
> **Rules:** Automated scanners must be rate-limited and are permitted only after notifying the team. Create no more than five test accounts. Do not access data belonging to other users. Do not perform destructive actions or install persistence. Use `test@example-lab.test` style accounts.
>
> **Disclosure:** Keep findings confidential until Example Corp confirms remediation. Public disclosure requires written permission.
>
> **Safe harbor:** Example Corp will not pursue legal action against researchers who follow this policy in good faith.

**Exercise:** Identify (a) a wildcard ambiguity, (b) a third-party exclusion, (c) a restriction on automation, and (d) a data-safety rule. Then list three questions you would ask before testing `beta.example-lab.test`.

## 13.10 Legal and Ethical Boundaries

- Unauthorized access is illegal in most jurisdictions (India IT Act 2000 + amendments; UK Computer Misuse Act 1990; US CFAA; equivalents elsewhere).
- Safe harbor clauses reduce risk but do not override law and are not blanket immunity.
- This book provides general education, not legal advice.
- If a test could damage data or affect other users, do not perform it.

## 13.11 Pre-Test Checklist

```
[ ] I have read the CURRENT program policy.
[ ] The asset is explicitly in scope (and not excluded).
[ ] My technique is allowed (no DoS/social engineering/etc.).
[ ] I am using my own test accounts / synthetic data.
[ ] My tools are rate-limited and non-destructive.
[ ] I know where and how to submit.
[ ] I understand the disclosure/confidentiality terms.
[ ] I have a research log open to record everything.
```

## 13.14 Mastery Deep Dive: Policy Anatomy and Scope Resolution

> **Operating premise.** A policy is the boundary of your permission. Every procedure below is to be applied only where you own the asset, are working in a lab, or hold a current written program authorization. When a clause is ambiguous, the answer is a written question, never an assumption.

### 13.14.1 Reading a Policy as a Structured Document

A policy is not prose to skim; it is a set of clauses with different authority. Read it in this order and record each category in your journal:

| Read order | Clause category | Question to answer | If missing |
| — | — | — | — |
| 1 | Authorization / safe harbor | What exactly am I permitted to do, and who grants it? | Do not test; ask in writing |
| 2 | Scope (inclusions) | Which hosts, apps, IPs, versions, and asset types are named? | Do not test; ask |
| 3 | Scope (exclusions) | Which subdomains, third parties, products, or bug classes are excluded? | Ask before testing anything ambiguous |
| 4 | Technique restrictions | Which methods are prohibited or require approval? | Default to conservative behavior; ask |
| 5 | Data handling | What accounts and data may I use? | Use only synthetic data; ask about accounts |
| 6 | Disclosure / embargo | What may I say, when, and to whom? | Assume confidentiality; ask |
| 7 | Rewards and severity | How are findings classified and compensated? | Does not affect permission |
| 8 | Contact and process | Where do I ask, report, and clarify? | Do not test until a channel exists |

**Precedence rule.** When clauses conflict, the more specific and more restrictive clause controls your behavior. A general statement like "we welcome security research" never overrides a specific exclusion like "staging is out of scope." Similarly, a platform's generic training material never overrides a program's explicit restriction.

### 13.14.2 Clause-by-Clause Annotation (Fictional Teaching Example)

Consider this fictional policy fragment:

> "In scope: `*.example-lab.test` and `app.example-lab.test`, including the public web application and its documented REST API. Out of scope: `support.example-lab.test` (third-party hosted), any asset not owned or operated by Example Corp, and all acquisitions not listed here. Automated scanners require 48-hour notice and a documented rate limit. Create no more than three test accounts. Do not access data belonging to other users. Keep findings confidential until we confirm remediation."

| Clause | Plain-language meaning | Operational rule for your journal |
| — | — | — |
| `*.example-lab.test` | Subdomains directly under the root; depth unspecified | Ask whether deeper levels like `a.b.example-lab.test` are included |
| `app.example-lab.test` | Explicitly named host | Safe to test if ownership confirmed |
| "public web application and documented REST API" | Application layer, documented endpoints | Undocumented internal APIs need a question |
| `support.example-lab.test` (third-party hosted) | Branded but not owned | Excluded; do not test |
| "any asset not owned or operated" | Ownership constraint | Verify before testing; ask when uncertain |
| "acquisitions not listed" | New subsidiaries are out | Do not infer inclusion from branding |
| "48-hour notice and rate limit" | Scanner approval required | No automated scanning until acknowledged |
| "no more than three test accounts" | Account ceiling | Track count; do not exceed |
| "do not access data belonging to other users" | Data boundary | Own accounts and synthetic values only |
| "confidential until remediation" | Disclosure embargo | No blog, post, or hint before permission |

Notice that the reward clause is absent from this fragment — and that its absence changes nothing about what you may test. Economics never expands authorization.

### 13.14.3 Wildcard Scope Interpretation

Wildcards are the most common source of accidental out-of-scope testing. Resolve them explicitly:

```text
Wildcard depth examples (assume root = example-lab.test):

  *.example-lab.test
    includes:  www.example-lab.test, api.example-lab.test
    ambiguous: a.b.example-lab.test  (two labels deep)
    depends:   CNAME to a SaaS provider (ownership may be third party)

  *.*.example-lab.test
    includes:  a.b.example-lab.test
    excludes:  a.example-lab.test (unless separately listed)

  example-lab.test
    often means apex only; does NOT automatically include subdomains
```

Four checks before treating a wildcard as permission:

1. **Depth.** Does the policy define how many labels are covered? If not, ask.
2. **Ownership.** Does the host resolve to infrastructure the program owns or operates? A CNAME to a third-party platform means the platform may own the service.
3. **Exclusion precedence.** Is the specific host named in an exclusion list? A specific exclusion beats a general wildcard.
4. **Acquisition status.** Is the host part of a recently acquired entity not yet listed? Treat as out of scope until named.

**Passive ownership-check workflow (no active testing of third parties):**

```bash
# Observational DNS lookups only; these answer "who does this point to?",
# they are not authorization to probe the resulting host.
dig +short api.example-lab.test CNAME
dig +short api.example-lab.test A
whois example-lab.test | grep -i -E 'registrant|org|country'
curl -s 'https://crt.sh/?q=%25.example-lab.test&output=json' \
  | jq -r '.[].name_value' | sort -u
```

Interpretation: a CNAME ending in a cloud or SaaS provider domain is a *signal to ask*, not a green light. A certificate name matching the program's domain is supportive evidence but not proof of current ownership. The authoritative answer is the program's written clarification.

### 13.14.4 A Taxonomy of Out-of-Scope Exclusions

| Exclusion type | Typical example | Why it exists | Your rule |
| — | — | — | — |
| Third-party hosted | Branded helpdesk on a vendor platform | Vendor owns the service and its rules | Never test; ask if branding confuses you |
| Environment | `dev`, `staging`, `internal` subdomains | Fragile, shared, or production-adjacent | Excluded unless explicitly listed |
| Product / brand | A subsidiary's separate product | Different owner or program | Out until named in the current policy |
| Acquisition | Recently acquired company's assets | Legal ownership and program coverage lag | Ask; never infer |
| Vulnerability class | DoS, social engineering, spam, physical | Harm to users and operations | Hard prohibition |
| Data type | Real customer records, payment data | Privacy and legal obligations | Do not access; stop on exposure |
| Technique | High-volume scanning, fuzzing without approval | Availability and noise | Get written approval first |
| Time window | Event-only scope | Authorization is bounded in time | Stop when the window closes |

### 13.14.5 The Ownership Verification Procedure

Use this checklist whenever a host is covered only by a wildcard or when branding and infrastructure disagree:

```text
[ ] The exact hostname appears in the current policy or under a defined wildcard.
[ ] No exclusion list names this hostname or its parent service.
[ ] DNS records (passively observed) point to infrastructure plausibly
    owned or operated by the program.
[ ] A third-party CNAME or cloud service has been identified and either
    accepted by the policy or clarified in writing.
[ ] The acquisition/subsidiary status is known.
[ ] The clarification question, if any, was answered in writing and dated.
[ ] The decision (test / do not test) is recorded in the journal.
```

If any box cannot be checked, the answer is **do not test yet**. This is not excessive caution; it is the professional standard that keeps bug bounty legal.

## 13.15 Mastery Deep Dive: Restrictions, Safe Harbor, and Disclosure

### 13.15.1 Testing Restrictions Decoded Into Operational Rules

Policies phrase restrictions broadly. Translate them into concrete behavior before you begin.

| Policy phrase | Operational translation |
| — | — |
| "No denial of service" | No volumetric floods, no resource-exhaustion payloads, no mass account creation, no recursive loops, no expensive queries at scale |
| "No social engineering" | No phishing, no phone calls to staff, no physical entry, no impersonation of employees or vendors |
| "Automated scanning requires approval" | No scanner or fuzzer run until you have written acknowledgement and agreed limits |
| "Do not access other users' data" | Only your labeled test accounts; if a response reveals real data, stop and do not store it |
| "Use provided test accounts" | Do not self-register outside the stated allowance; do not use purchased or credential-stuffed accounts |
| "No destructive payloads" | No file deletion, no data modification on real records, no persistence, no lateral movement |
| "Respect rate limits" | Keep request volumes low, back off on errors, avoid parallel bursts |
| "Maintain confidentiality" | No public writeups, screenshots, hints, or discussion until permission |
| "Stop if you encounter real data" | Cease the specific request stream and report the observation without further access |

**Annotated mistake.** A researcher finds an endpoint that accepts a large `page_size` value. Curiosity prompts them to request a million records "just to see." Even if nothing is saved, this is a resource-exhaustion test, likely a DoS violation, and it risks pulling real user data into the response. The professional approach is to note the unbounded parameter as a hypothesis and ask the program whether a controlled, rate-limited validation is permitted.

### 13.15.2 A Data-Handling Standard

Data safety is a discipline with four rules:

1. **Minimize by construction.** Use synthetic values (`TEST-A-2026`), your own accounts, and the smallest object set that proves the boundary violation.
2. **Stop on discovery.** If a response contains real user data, stop that request stream, do not copy, screenshot, or store the value, and note only the field type and observation.
3. **Redact evidence.** Screenshots and logs must show markers, not names, emails, tokens belonging to others, or production identifiers that identify real people.
4. **Retain responsibly.** Keep local evidence only as long as needed for the report; store it where only you can access it; delete when the engagement and any allowed retention period end.

```markdown
# Data-Safety Log (per session)

- Accounts used (synthetic labels only):
- Markers used:
- Any real-data exposure observed? yes / no
  - If yes: request stream stopped at (time/step):
  - What was redacted from notes:
  - Reported through authorized channel? yes / no
- Evidence stored locally at:
- Retention end date:
```

### 13.15.3 Safe Harbor and Legal Language

Safe harbor is conditional permission, not immunity. Parse it with this checklist:

```text
[ ] Who grants it? (the asset owner, a parent company, or both?)
[ ] Who is protected? (named individuals, registered researchers,
    "anyone acting in good faith"?)
[ ] What conduct is protected? (testing within scope using allowed methods)
[ ] What conditions attach? (data handling, stop rules, rate limits)
[ ] What is expressly excluded? (third parties, out-of-scope assets,
    criminal conduct)
[ ] Does it bind affiliates and acquisitions? (often no)
[ ] Does it say anything about statutory law? (a private clause cannot
    amend a statute)
[ ] Is there a named contact for clarification?
```

**Interpreting common phrases:**

- "Good faith" means honest adherence to the policy — not merely lacking malicious intent. Ignoring an exclusion is not good faith.
- "We will not pursue legal action" binds only the issuing entity; it says nothing about other companies or authorities.
- "Does not constitute consent to activity outside scope" means you must point to a specific in-scope clause for every action.
- "Subject to applicable law" is a reminder that statutes still apply.

This chapter is education, not legal advice. If an engagement raises a legal question you cannot resolve from the policy and a written clarification, do not proceed.

### 13.15.4 Disclosure and Embargo Rules

Disclosure clauses answer *when and how* you may speak. Treat them as a timeline with gates:

```text
Found issue
   |
   v
Report submitted privately -- confidential from this moment
   |
   v
Triage confirms / requests info -- still confidential
   |
   v
Remediation confirmed by program
   |
   +-- policy permits disclosure? -- no  -> remain silent unless written permission
   |
   +-- policy permits disclosure? -- yes -> coordinate date and content
                                          |
                                          v
                              Publish only agreed details at agreed time
```

**What "confidential" typically prohibits:** blog posts, social media hints, conference talks, writeups, sharing PoCs with third parties, and discussing the issue with anyone outside the permitted channel. Even a statement like "I found a serious bug in a well-known company" can violate an embargo if it reveals enough to identify the target.

**When no program exists**, the general responsible-disclosure sequence in 13.8 applies: report privately, allow a reasonable remediation window, communicate professionally, coordinate publication, and consider a coordinator such as a CERT if the vendor is unresponsive. Follow applicable law throughout.

### 13.15.5 Red Flags in a Program

| Red flag | Why it is dangerous | Recommended action |
| — | — | — |
| No safe harbor or vague authorization | You cannot point to permission | Do not test; request written clarification |
| "All our assets" with no list | Unbounded and unverifiable scope | Ask for an exact asset list |
| Wildcards with no depth definition | Easy to stray out of scope | Ask before testing sub-subdomains |
| No exclusions list | Third parties likely included by accident | Ask which hosts are third-party |
| Rewards far above norms with no rules | Often a sign of an immature or misleading program | Apply extra scrutiny; verify policy language |
| Triage comments that blame researchers | Poor-faith process | Limit time investment or avoid |
| Requirements to access real user data | Privacy and legal violation | Refuse; report the concern |
| No disclosure contact | Cannot resolve ambiguity | Do not test |
| Scope changes without notice | Yesterday's permission is invalid today | Re-read policy before every session |

## 13.16 Mastery Deep Dive: Scope Maps, Questions, and Boundary Discipline

### 13.16.1 Building a Written Scope Map

A scope map converts prose into a decision artifact you can use under time pressure. Build one per program before testing.

```markdown
# Scope Map: <Program Name>

Policy URL:
Policy read on (date):
Contact for questions:

## Authorized
- Exact hosts / apps / IPs / asset types:
- Wildcard strings and defined depth:
- Asset types (web, API, mobile, source, on-chain):
- Time window (if any):

## Not Authorized
- Named excluded hosts:
- Third-party hosts identified (with observed CNAME):
- Excluded vulnerability classes:
- Excluded techniques:
- Data types off limits:

## Ambiguities and Their Resolution
| Asset / question | Asked on | Answer received | Decision |
| — | — | — | — |
| | | | |

## Operational Constraints
- Test accounts and count limit:
- Scanner approval and rate limit:
- Data markers:
- Disclosure terms:

## Decision Summary
Green (test freely within rules):
Amber (ask first):
Red (never test):
```

**Worked example (fictional).** For the policy in 13.14.2, the Green list contains `app.example-lab.test` and direct subdomains of `example-lab.test` whose ownership is confirmed. The Amber list contains `a.b.example-lab.test` (undefined wildcard depth) and any API not in public documentation. The Red list contains `support.example-lab.test` (third-party), all acquisitions, all real user data, DoS, social engineering, and unapproved scanners. With this map, a testing decision takes seconds instead of guesswork.

### 13.16.2 How to Ask Scope Questions

A good scope question is short, specific, and answerable. It quotes the clause, names the asset, and asks for a binary decision.

**Template:**

```text
Subject: Scope clarification for <program> — <asset>

Hello,
I am planning authorized testing under your current policy (read on <date>).
Your policy states: "<quote the exact clause>".
I would like to confirm: is <exact hostname / asset / technique> included
in scope for this program?

Context: <one sentence, e.g., passive DNS shows a CNAME to a third-party
provider, and I want to avoid testing an out-of-scope asset.>

A yes/no answer is sufficient. Thank you for the clarification.
```

**Weak vs. strong examples:**

- Weak: "Can I test your subdomains?" — too broad; invites an ambiguous answer.
- Strong: "Does `*.example-lab.test` include two-label subdomains such as `a.b.example-lab.test`, and is `beta.example-lab.test` covered given it is not listed in exclusions?"
- Weak: "Is scanning okay?" — misses rate and approval details.
- Strong: "The policy requires 48-hour notice for automated scanners. If I notify you with the target host, tool, and a one-request-per-second limit, is that sufficient approval?"

Always record the answer, date, and the name or role of the person who gave it in your scope map. An answer you did not write down did not happen.

### 13.16.3 Ambiguous-Asset Decision Tree

```text
Is the asset explicitly named in the policy?
   yes -> is it also named in an exclusion?
             yes -> RED: do not test.
             no  -> is a required technique restricted?
                       yes -> get written approval or change technique.
                       no  -> GREEN: test within all other rules.
   no  -> is it covered by a wildcard whose depth is defined?
             yes -> does ownership verify (13.14.5)?
                       yes -> GREEN.
                       no  -> AMBER: ask about ownership.
             no  -> is it branded as belonging to the program?
                       yes -> AMBER: branding is not ownership; ask.
                       no  -> RED: do not test.
```

If you ever find yourself trying to talk your way around a RED outcome, stop the session. That impulse is the single most common precursor to an authorization incident.

### 13.16.4 Common Boundary Mistakes (Annotated)

1. **"It has the company name, so it is theirs."** A support portal on a vendor platform may be branded but operated by a third party. Check the CNAME and ask.
2. **"The wildcard obviously includes it."** Wildcards may be one label only. Verify depth in the policy.
3. **"The policy was like that last month."** Policies change. Re-read before each session and record the date.
4. **"I will only look, not test."** Active probing of an out-of-scope host is still testing. Passive observation is fine; requests that probe behavior are not.
5. **"My scan is small, so the no-scanning rule does not apply."** Restrictions are categorical unless an approval path is stated.
6. **"I will use the data only to prove the bug."** Accessing real user data is prohibited regardless of intent; use synthetic accounts.
7. **"The reward is high, so the risk must be acceptable."** Economics never changes scope or law.
8. **"I disclosed only the hostname."** Confidentiality clauses can cover identifying details; if in doubt, say nothing until permission.

### 13.16.5 What Mastery Looks Like

```text
[ ] I produce a written scope map before my first request to a target.
[ ] I can quote the authorization clause that covers each action I take.
[ ] I verify wildcard depth and ownership instead of assuming either.
[ ] I treat acquisitions and third-party hosts as out of scope until named.
[ ] I ask short, quotable, binary scope questions and record the answers.
[ ] I translate every restriction into concrete "never do" behavior.
[ ] I use only synthetic data and stop immediately on real-data exposure.
[ ] I keep all findings confidential until the policy permits disclosure.
[ ] I recognize red flags and decline programs that cannot resolve them.
[ ] I never let reward size influence an authorization decision.
```

## 13.12 Key Takeaways

- Scope and rules are the boundary of permission; ambiguity means ask, not assume.
- Restrictions on DoS, automation, data access, and accounts exist to protect systems and users.
- A safe PoC uses your own accounts and demonstrates the boundary violation with minimal impact.
- Authorization, data safety, and legality are absolute constraints.
